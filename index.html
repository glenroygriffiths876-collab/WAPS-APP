Parent lessons

Short, respectful ways to help during play and everyday routines. Home should still feel like home.

Best teaching formula:
Follow the child → create a reason to communicate → wait → notice any attempt → respond immediately → add one small model.
What counts as communication?

Looking toward an item, reaching, moving closer or away, facial expression, gesture, picture, sign, sound, word, typing and behaviour can all communicate. The adult’s first job is to understand the message.

Should I require speech?

No. Model speech while accepting any effective communication. Never withhold food, toilet access, comfort, help or a needed break until a child says a word.

Should I stop stimming?

Not when it is harmless. Repetitive movement may support regulation or joy. Address safety, pain or severe interference by changing the environment and teaching alternatives—not by demanding “quiet hands.”

Understanding difficult moments

What your child does may be a message. Look for what happened, what your child may need, and what can help next time.

During a meltdown: reduce language, reduce demands, create space, protect safety, offer the child’s known supports that help the child feel safe and wait. Do not lecture, shame, threaten, restrain, film or demand an apology while the child is overwhelmed.

What happened before and after?

Write what happened just before, what you saw or heard, and what happened right after. Use clear words, not labels such as “naughty.”

Prevention planner

1. Make the trigger smaller
Shorter wait, quieter place, fewer words, easier first step.
2. Add predictability
Visual schedule, first–then, countdown, transition object.
3. Teach a replacement
“Break,” “help,” “more time,” “finished,” “too loud,” “pain.”
4. Reinforce the replacement
Respond quickly when the child uses the safer communication.
5. Review health
Sudden behaviour changes can reflect pain, illness, sleep, dental, gastrointestinal, hearing or other concerns.

Shadows and school support

Clear steps for parents and for people who want to work as a school shadow.

Important: A shadow is not a babysitter or the child’s private teacher. The shadow helps the child take part in school and should slowly reduce help as independence grows.

How to ask for shadow support

  1. Write to the principal. Explain your child’s strengths, barriers and the exact help needed.
  2. Ask for a Student Support Team meeting. Bring useful reports, but share only what the school needs.
  3. Ask for a written support plan. Include communication, safety, breaks, learning changes and how progress will be reviewed.
  4. Ask the school to contact the Regional Special Needs Coordinator. Region 4 serves St. James, Hanover and Westmoreland.
  5. Review the support. The shadow should increase participation, not separate the child from the class.
Special Education UnitOfficial special education brochureCall Region 4: 876-953-6928

What good shadow support looks like

✅ Uses the child’s Talk Board or communication system.
✅ Helps the child join the class and peers.
✅ Uses the least help needed.
✅ Plans for breaks and sensory needs.
✅ Keeps information private.
✅ Works with the teacher and family.
Not okay: forcing eye contact, removing AAC, doing the child’s work, isolating the child, punishment, humiliation or unnecessary restraint.

How to prepare to work as a shadow

We did not find one permanent public application form for all shadow jobs in Jamaica. Vacancies may be handled by the Ministry, a school, a family or a service provider.

  1. Prepare a short CV and two references.
  2. Get training in special needs support, communication, safeguarding and first aid.
  3. Apply for a police record when required.
  4. Check the Ministry recruitment site and contact schools or Regional Special Needs Coordinators.
  5. Ask for written duties, supervision and a plan for reducing prompts.
MOEY jobs portalHEART Special Needs Teacher Assistant coursePolice record appointments

Skills a shadow needs

  • Patience and respectful communication
  • Ability to follow a child’s support plan
  • Understanding of AAC and visual supports
  • Safe support during distress
  • Clear note-taking
  • Confidentiality and child safeguarding
  • Ability to accept supervision and feedback

Set up the role clearly

  • Name the supervising teacher.
  • Give the shadow the child’s communication and safety plan.
  • Agree what information can be shared with the family.
  • Use least-to-most help and fade prompts.
  • Review goals at set dates.
  • Plan backup support when the shadow is absent.

Protect inclusion

The teacher remains responsible for teaching. The shadow supports access, but should not become a wall between the child and classmates.

Evidence and scope

This companion draws on principles used in caregiver-mediated developmental support, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC), responsive interaction, visual supports, functional communication and positive behaviour support. It intentionally excludes aversive punishment, forced compliance and claims of an autism “cure.”

Key public sources linked in the app include the World Health Organization Caregiver Skills Training materials, the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association’s AAC guidance, Jamaica’s Ministry of Labour and Social Security, JCPD, and the Ministry of Education Special Education Unit. Resource details should be rechecked periodically.
